AI and Legal Ethics

The 2026 case law on AI chats: when they are privileged or protected work product, when they are not, and what courts are writing into protective orders. 26 decisions, each with the order.

Four separate questions

They get blurred together. They have different rules.

1Confidentiality

The ethical duty (Rule 1.6). Covers all information relating to the representation, not just privileged communications.

2Attorney-client privilege

Confidential lawyer-client communications for legal advice. Waived by disclosure to any third party.

3Work product

Material prepared for litigation, by or for a party. Waived only by disclosure to an adversary, or in a way likely to reach one.

4Protective orders

Imposed by the court in a case. Govern what you may do with the other side’s confidential documents.

Three questions decide protection

Tool or third party?

Most courts: a tool (“tools, not persons,” Warner). Heppner: a third party, so privileged information put into it is waived.

Did a lawyer direct the use?

Most civil courts: not needed for work product (Morgan, Tate, Fontainebleau). Contra: Heppner, Shealy.

Do the account terms keep it confidential?

Consumer terms counted against confidentiality in Heppner and Whisman (privilege) and Fry (sealing). Morgan: no automatic waiver.

Where Heppner goes too far

My view. The result may be right; he used Claude on his own, and Claude isn’t a lawyer. Three parts of the reasoning go too far.

Treating AI like a person

Footnote 3 treats Claude as a third party, so putting privileged information in waives it. Nobody asks whether Microsoft Word is a lawyer.

Consumer vs. enterprise terms

Westlaw and Microsoft 365 don’t promise absolute confidentiality either. The ethics standard for cloud services has long been reasonable care, not an ironclad guarantee.

Must a lawyer direct the use?

This is the real split. Most civil cases say no, for work product. Shealy, the romantic-partner case, is the outlier.

Practical adviceClients shouldn’t run their matter through any chatbot without talking to counsel. If AI is used, counsel directs it, in writing.

Privilege and work product for a party’s own AI chats.

United States v. Heppner

No privilege · No work product
S.D.N.Y. · Feb. 17, 2026 · Rakoff, J.

Judge Rakoff held that a represented criminal defendant's own exchanges with the consumer version of Claude were protected by neither the attorney-client privilege nor the work product doctrine. Claude is not a lawyer, Anthropic's consumer privacy policy defeated any reasonable expectation of confidentiality, and Heppner acted on his own rather than at counsel's direction. It is the leading S.D.N.Y. decision on the issue, and New York courts are already citing it, including the Assini court in Nassau County.

United States v. Heppner, No. 25 Cr. 503 (JSR), ECF No. 27 (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 17, 2026) (Rakoff, J.) (memorandum giving reasons for Feb. 10, 2026 bench ruling); 2026 WL 436479; 2026 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 32697. Secondary sources and Heppner's own later briefing cite it as 820 F. Supp. 3d 292; the reporter pages were not checked.

Why it matters

The case everyone has heard of, and the only decision so far to say that putting privileged information into an AI tool waives the privilege (footnote 3). Read it for its three facts: a client acting on his own, a consumer account, and no lawyer direction. Most later civil courts have declined to follow its work-product reasoning.

Facts

Bradley Heppner, an executive of GWG Holdings and related entities, was indicted in October 2025 for securities fraud, wire fraud, false statements to auditors and related crimes. The indictment alleges that he defrauded GWG investors of more than $150 million. When the FBI arrested him on November 4, 2025, agents executed a search warrant at his home and seized documents and devices. Among the seized materials were about thirty-one documents memorializing Heppner's exchanges with Claude, Anthropic's generative AI platform. Defense counsel said the exchanges took place in 2025, after Heppner had received a grand jury subpoena and knew he was a target. In them Heppner, without any suggestion from counsel, prepared reports outlining defense strategy and the arguments he might make on the facts and the law. Heppner claimed privilege on three grounds: he had put information learned from counsel into Claude, he created the documents in order to talk to his lawyers, and he later shared them with counsel. Counsel conceded, however, that they "did not direct [Heppner] to run Claude searches." Under a privilege protocol stipulation the government segregated the documents unread. It moved on February 6, 2026 for a ruling that they were not protected. Judge Rakoff granted the motion from the bench on February 10 and issued this memorandum on February 17.

Holding

The AI Documents were not protected by the attorney-client privilege or the work product doctrine, so the government could review them. The court found that the documents lacked "at least two, if not all three" elements of the privilege: they were not communications with a lawyer, they were not confidential, and they were not made to obtain legal advice. On work product, the court assumed the documents were prepared in anticipation of litigation. It held them unprotected anyway, because they were not prepared by or at the behest of counsel and did not reflect counsel's strategy.

Reasoning

On privilege, the court began with the Second Circuit's three elements (United States v. Mejia). The first element failed because Claude is not an attorney, and the court said that "alone disposes" of the claim. The court then addressed commentators' argument that AI inputs are less like communications and more like using cloud-based word processing software. It rejected the analogy. Using such software "is not intrinsically privileged in any case," and the comparison actually cuts against privilege. Recognized privileges depend on a trusting human relationship with a licensed professional who owes fiduciary duties and is subject to discipline, and no such relationship can exist with Claude. The court did not hold that using cloud software waives privilege. It held only that the software analogy cannot create a privilege that does not otherwise exist.

The second element, confidentiality, failed. The court said this was "not merely" because Heppner used a third-party platform. It was "also" because Anthropic's consumer privacy policy, as of February 2025, told users that Anthropic collects inputs and outputs, uses them to train Claude, and may disclose them to third parties, including governmental regulatory authorities and parties to litigation, even without a subpoena. The court also relied on a January 5, 2026 order in the In re OpenAI copyright MDL in the same District. Unlike notes a client prepares to give a lawyer, Heppner "first shared the equivalent of his notes with a third-party, Claude." The third element, purpose of obtaining legal advice, was a "closer call." Counsel had not directed the AI use, so what mattered was whether Heppner sought legal advice from Claude, and Claude disclaims giving legal advice. The court suggested that had counsel directed the use, Claude might arguably have functioned like a Kovel-type agent. Sending the outputs to counsel afterward did not make them privileged (Gould). In footnote 3, answering an argument counsel made "in passing" at oral argument, the court added an alternative point. Even if some inputs repeated privileged advice, Heppner waived the privilege by sharing it with Claude and Anthropic, "just as if he had shared it with any other third party."

On work product, the court relied on the Second Circuit's formulation that the doctrine protects material prepared "by or at the behest of counsel." Counsel confirmed that Heppner prepared the documents "on his own volition." Counsel also conceded that the documents "affect" but did not "reflect" counsel's strategy at the time. The court rejected Heppner's reliance on Fed. R. Crim. P. 16(b)(2)(A), because the documents were seized under a warrant, not demanded in discovery. It also expressly disagreed with Shih v. Petal Card (S.D.N.Y. 2021), which had extended work product protection without attorney direction. Rakoff reasoned that the doctrine exists to protect lawyers' mental processes.

Key quotations

  • “Because Claude is not an attorney, see ECF No. 23-6, that alone disposes of Heppner’s claim of privilege.” (slip op. at 5)
  • “But the use of such applications is not intrinsically privileged in any case” (slip op. at 5 (rejecting the cloud-based word processing analogy))
  • “This is not merely because Heppner communicated with a third-party AI platform but also because the written privacy policy to which users of Claude consent provides that Anthropic collects data on both users’ “inputs” and Claude’s “outputs,” that it uses such data to “train” Claude, and that Anthropic reserves the right to disclose such data to a host of “third parties,” including “governmental regulatory authorities.”” (slip op. at 6)
  • “Had counsel directed Heppner to use Claude, Claude might arguably be said to have functioned in a manner akin to a highly trained professional who may act as a lawyer’s agent within the protection of the attorney-client privilege.” (slip op. at 7)
  • “But even if certain information that Heppner input into Claude was privileged, he waived the privilege by sharing that information with Claude and Anthropic, just as if he had shared it with any other third party.” (slip op. at 8 n.3)
  • “Because the AI Documents were not prepared at the behest of counsel and did not disclose counsel’s strategy, they do not merit protection as work product.” (slip op. at 12)

Limits

Do not read footnote 3's waiver sentence as the holding. It answers an argument made in passing and is offered in the alternative. The principal grounds are that Claude is not a lawyer, that the consumer privacy policy defeated confidentiality, and that the use was not for legal advice. The court did not hold that using AI, or cloud software, waives privilege; it held that the software analogy cannot create a privilege that does not otherwise exist. The work product ruling rests on the absence of counsel's involvement, not on disclosure to Anthropic. This is a criminal case decided on documents seized by warrant, and civil courts (Morgan, Tate, Assini) have distinguished it on that ground. It has not been reviewed on appeal.

Later history

Heppner was convicted at trial. His post-trial motion argued that the AI documents were work product and that using Claude did not waive it. On Oct. 13, 2026 (ECF 80) the court denied the motion “in its entirety” and said an opinion giving its reasons “will issue shortly”; none had issued as of the last docket check (late October). New counsel (Shapiro Arato Bach) appeared Oct. 20. Sentencing is set for Jan. 11, 2027; the AI ruling would be reviewable on appeal after judgment.

Warner v. Gilbarco, Inc.

Work product · No waiver
E.D. Mich. · Feb. 10, 2026 · Patti, M.J.

A federal magistrate judge refused to let defendants discover a pro se plaintiff's ChatGPT use. He held that her AI materials were her own work product, and that using ChatGPT did not waive protection because work product is waived only by disclosure to an adversary, and ChatGPT and other generative AI programs are "tools, not persons." It issued the same day as Heppner's bench ruling, a week before Rakoff's written memorandum, and it supplies the waiver framework that Morgan, Assini and Villanueva later adopted.

Warner v. Gilbarco, Inc., No. 2:24-cv-12333-GAD-APP, ECF No. 94 (E.D. Mich. Feb. 10, 2026) (Patti, M.J.); 2026 WL 373043; reported as 820 F. Supp. 3d 629 per secondary sources (the reporter pages were not checked).

Why it matters

The first opinion to say AI tools are "tools, not persons," and the source of the waiver rule most later courts use: work product is waived only by disclosure to an adversary, not by typing into a chatbot.

Facts

Sohyon Warner sued Gilbarco, Inc. and Vontier Corporation. Her later summary judgment filings show it is an employment discrimination and retaliation case. She was represented early on, but her counsel withdrew, and she litigated the discovery disputes on her own. Fact discovery closed December 5, 2025. On December 23, 2025, defendants moved to compel (ECF 80). Among other things, they sought "all documents and information concerning her use of third-party AI tools in connection with this lawsuit." They also asked the court to overrule her attorney-client and work product objections to the "AI materials," or alternatively to require a privilege log. The court notes that defendants had questioned Warner at length about AI at her deposition, without uncovering any suggestion that she had uploaded confidential discovery to an AI platform. Magistrate Judge Patti heard the motion in person on February 5, 2026 and ruled on February 10, 2026. The same order denied Warner's own sanctions motion, after in camera review of the defendants' internal investigation reports.

Holding

The AI part of the motion was denied. The motion as a whole was untimely because it was filed after the close of discovery without good cause. Alternatively, the AI materials were not discoverable. They are documents prepared in anticipation of litigation by a party, protected by Rule 26(b)(3)(A), and they were not relevant or, at best, not proportional. Even if they were discoverable, they were work product, which a pro se litigant may assert. Using ChatGPT did not waive the protection. The court also refused to require a privilege log for the AI materials. The only relief granted on defendants' motion concerned signed HIPAA releases for medical providers.

Reasoning

The court first relied on the text of Rule 26(b)(3)(A), emphasizing (with the court's own italics) that the rule protects materials prepared "by another party or its representative," not only by lawyers. It cited district court authority holding that a pro se litigant may claim work product. On waiver, the court drew the traditional distinction between the two protections. Voluntary disclosure to any third party generally waives the attorney-client privilege. Work product, by contrast, is waived only by disclosure to an adversary or in a way likely to put the material in an adversary's hands (In re Columbia/HCA, 6th Cir., and United States v. AT&T, D.C. Cir.). The court then held that ChatGPT and other generative AI programs are "tools, not persons," even if administrators are somewhere in the background. Typing into them therefore is not disclosure to an adversary.

The court also adopted the plaintiff's framing of what defendants were really after. It described the motion as an attempt to compel her internal analysis and thought process rather than any existing evidence. It called the motion a "fishing expedition" and "a distraction from the merits." It noted that defendants' theory was supported by no case law, only a Law360 article posing rhetorical questions. And it agreed that the theory "would nullify work-product protection in nearly every modern drafting environment." In a footnote the court noted there was no evidence Warner had violated the protective order by uploading confidential documents to AI. It added that "Defendants' preoccupation with Plaintiff's use of AI needs to abate." The order closes by saying that both sides sought each other's thought processes and the court would order production of neither.

Key quotations

  • “Even if this information were discoverable, it is subject to protection under the work-product doctrine, which Plaintiff is permitted to assert.” (slip op. at 11)
  • “Moreover, to the extent Defendants argue that Plaintiff waived the work-product protection by using ChatGPT, the work-product waiver has to be a waiver to an adversary or in a way likely to get in an adversary’s hand.” (slip op. at 11)
  • “And ChatGPT (and other generative AI programs) are tools, not persons, even if they may have administrators somewhere in the background.” (slip op. at 12)
  • “would nullify work-product protection in nearly every modern drafting environment, a result no court has endorsed.” (slip op. at 12–13 (court agreeing with and quoting plaintiff's brief))
  • “Defendants’ preoccupation with Plaintiff’s use of AI needs to abate.” (slip op. at 10 n.3)
  • “The Court will uphold the protections afforded the thought processes and litigation strategies of both sides and will order production of neither.” (slip op. at 14)

Limits

Warner is a short discovery order by a magistrate judge in a pro se case. The AI ruling is an alternative ground: the motion was first denied as untimely. The analysis is brief, about two pages, and does not address the AI provider's terms of service at all. Do not attribute to Warner the sentence that conditioning protection on counsel's involvement "finds no support in the rule's text"; that is Morgan's. Warner does not address the attorney-client privilege in substance. Its only nod to privilege is quoting the rule that voluntary disclosure to a third party waives it, to contrast work product. It does not hold that AI use by a represented party is protected without counsel's direction.

Later history

No objection to ECF 94 appears on the docket. On Oct. 19, 2026 the district judge granted summary judgment to the defendants on the employment claims; that opinion does not revisit the AI ruling. Morgan, Tate, Assini, Whisman and Villanueva all follow Warner's waiver reasoning; Heppner's post-trial motion relied on it.

Morgan v. V2X, Inc.

Work product · No automatic waiver · AI clause added
D. Colo. · Mar. 30, 2026 · Dominguez Braswell, M.J.

A federal magistrate judge held that a pro se plaintiff's AI-assisted litigation work is protected work product under Rule 26(b)(3). Using a mainstream AI tool does not automatically destroy confidentiality or waive that protection. The plaintiff still had to disclose the name of the AI tool he used with the defendant's confidential documents. The court also amended the protective order to bar anyone from feeding confidential discovery material into an AI tool unless the provider is contractually barred from training on or disclosing it, and it required written documentation of those terms. Assini, the Nassau County decision, adopted Morgan's reasoning, which makes Morgan the most important out-of-state case for New York practice.

Morgan v. V2X, Inc., No. 1:25-cv-01991-SKC-MDB, ECF No. 65 (D. Colo. Mar. 30, 2026) (Dominguez Braswell, M.J.); 2026 WL 864223; 2026 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 67939 (as cited in Assini).

Why it matters

The most-followed civil decision. It protects a party's own AI work without a lawyer, rejects automatic waiver from a provider's data collection, and writes a model protective-order clause for the other side's confidential documents. New York's only decision (Assini) adopts its reasoning.

Facts

Archie Morgan, representing himself, sued his former employer V2X, Inc. He alleges a hostile work environment and a termination based on race and national origin and in retaliation for protected activity. The case already had a stipulated protective order that barred disclosure of material designated "Confidential" except in enumerated circumstances. Both sides were using AI in their litigation work. The dispute first surfaced when Morgan moved to compel production of an insurance policy. He claimed that V2X was holding the policy back as leverage to force him to agree to an AI amendment to the protective order. He also complained that barring a pro se litigant from "modern analytical aids" created a "technological gap," since defense counsel's firm had its own proprietary AI. The court struck that motion on procedural grounds, and the insurance dispute was resolved. V2X then moved (Doc. 52) for two things: an amended protective order with AI-specific restrictions, and an order compelling Morgan to identify the AI tool he was using so V2X could check whether its confidential information was safe. Morgan agreed the protective order should be amended but proposed different language. He opposed identifying his tool, arguing that his choice of software was itself work product.

Holding

The court granted V2X's motion in part. First, Rule 26(b)(3) applies to a pro se litigant's AI-assisted litigation materials. Using a mainstream AI tool does not by itself eliminate the expectation of privacy or waive the protection. Second, the identity of the AI tool is not protected on this record. Morgan had not shown how naming the tool would reveal his mental impressions or strategy, and V2X had a legitimate need to know where its confidential information had gone. Morgan was ordered to disclose, within ten days, the name of any AI platform he used to upload, process, analyze or store information V2X had designated Confidential. V2X then had ten days to tell the court whether it would seek relief. Third, the court rejected both sides' proposed language and wrote its own AI provision for an amended protective order. No one may input Confidential information into any AI platform unless the provider is contractually prohibited from (1) storing or using inputs to train or improve its model and (2) disclosing inputs to third parties except where essential to delivering the service. Any such third party must be bound by equally protective obligations. The provider must also contractually allow the user to delete the Confidential information on request. A party using an AI tool it believes meets these requirements must keep written documentation of the contractual protections.

Reasoning

On applicability, the court read the text of Rule 26(b)(3)(A), which protects materials prepared in anticipation of litigation "by or for another party or its representative." It also relied on the 1970 amendments, which extended protection beyond attorneys, and on cases applying the rule to pro se litigants. The court reasoned that AI makes this especially important for pro se litigants, who must act as both party and advocate. Conditioning protection on counsel's involvement, it said, "finds no support in the rule's text." It distinguished Heppner on two grounds. Heppner was a criminal case, while civil Rule 26(b)(3) protects a party's own work product. And in Heppner there was a gap between the client and his lawyer that does not exist when the party is his own advocate.

On confidentiality and waiver, the court acknowledged that ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and similar public tools collect user data for training. It held that this does not eliminate all expectation of privacy. It drew on Fourth Amendment cases (Warshak and Carpenter) for the principle that routing information through a third-party system does not forfeit all privacy. It noted that conversational AI invites candid disclosure. It concluded that it is "entirely reasonable" to expect some privacy when using these tools. It then held that work product protection is waived only by disclosure to an adversary or in circumstances that substantially increase the likelihood an adversary will obtain the material. Information given to an AI provider is "highly unlikely" to reach an adversary absent legal process. Here the court followed Warner's "tools, not persons" reasoning.

On scope, the court left open whether disclosing a tool could ever reveal strategy, citing Sporck v. Peil on selection as work product. It held that Morgan's conclusory assertions did not carry his burden. On the protective order, the court found two things. Morgan's "closed-circuit environment" language addressed cybersecurity rather than AI-specific risks. V2X's language was over-engineered to fit V2X's own AI contracts. The court acknowledged that its provision will, for now, bar the parties from using most, if not all, mainstream low-to-no-cost AI with Confidential information, and that this disadvantages pro se litigants. It cautioned the parties against over-designation and stressed that AI uses not involving Confidential information are unrestricted.

Key quotations

  • “A reading of Rule 26(b)(3) that conditions work product protection over AI materials on the involvement of counsel finds no support in the rule’s text and would further disadvantage unrepresented litigants.” (slip op. at 7)
  • “In other words, given how AI tools function, it is entirely reasonable for a person to expect some privacy and confidentiality when interacting with these tools, even though they understand a third party is behind the tool collecting and storing their information.” (slip op. at 10)
  • “In other words, even though AI use technically “discloses” information to a third party, it is highly unlikely the information will fall into the hands of an adversary absent some legal process to compel it.” (slip op. at 10)
  • “If Plaintiff already submitted Confidential Information to some AI system—and it appears he has—Defendant is entitled to know which system.” (slip op. at 12)
  • “A party intending to use AI that it contends meets these requirements must retain written documentation of these contractual protections.” (slip op. at 14–15 (amended protective order language))
  • “The Court recognizes that practically speaking, and in light of the current state of AI, this provision will (at least for now) bar the parties from using most, if not all, mainstream low-to-no-cost AI to process Confidential Information.” (slip op. at 15)

Limits

Morgan is a magistrate judge's order in a pro se civil case. Its reasoning leans heavily on the litigant acting as both party and advocate, and Shealy distinguished it on exactly that ground. It does not decide whether a represented party's self-directed AI use is protected. It addresses work product only, not the attorney-client privilege, so it does not directly contradict Heppner's privilege holding. The Fourth Amendment analogy is offered as "informative" only; the court acknowledges it is a different framework. The tool-identity ruling turned on a failure of proof, and the court left open that naming a tool could reveal strategy in another case. The "finds no support in the rule's text" sentence is Morgan's, not Warner's; do not attribute it to Warner. The same magistrate judge later declined, in Dunn v. LexisNexis Risk Solutions (D. Colo. Aug. 31, 2026), to adopt a per-tool "consent-and-amend" protective order clause. So her approach is contract-based safeguards, not case-by-case approval of each tool.

Later history

V2X filed a notice regarding the plaintiff's AI tools on Apr. 10, 2026 (ECF 69), apparently after the ordered disclosure. In May the court appointed limited-scope pro bono counsel for settlement and stayed discovery. No objection to or review of ECF 65 has been identified. Tym (D.N.M.), Tate (Tex.), Assini (N.Y.) and Fischman (N.D. Tex.) have since relied on it.

Tym v. Cerno

AI prompts protected (this case only)
D.N.M. · Apr. 22, 2026 · Rozzoni, M.J.

In a two-page discovery order, a magistrate judge adopted Morgan "at this time and for this case only" and held that a pro se plaintiff's generative AI interactions used to prepare filings or hearings are shielded by the work product doctrine. The question was raised at the hearing and never briefed.

Tym v. Cerno, No. 1:25-cv-00498-JCH-JMR, ECF No. 46 (D.N.M. Apr. 22, 2026) (Rozzoni, M.J.) (order granting motion to compel).

Why it matters

Shows how quickly Morgan became the default answer for pro se litigants, even without briefing.

Facts

Miguel Tym, representing himself, sued state officials and the New Mexico Health Care Authority. Defendants moved to compel discovery on a range of interrogatories and document requests. At the April 17 hearing, defendants also asked for a ruling on whether prompts posed to a generative AI product "such as ChatGPT, Claude, etc." are protected work product.

Holding

The motion to compel was granted, and the plaintiff was ordered to amend his responses. His work product objection to one document request (RFP 10) was sustained, but he had to serve a privilege log for what he withheld. On the AI question, the court adopted Morgan's reasoning for this case only: "if Plaintiff used generative AI to prepare his filings or for hearings in this matter, those interactions are shielded by the work product doctrine."

Reasoning

The order gives no independent analysis. It notes that case law is limited and the issue was not briefed, quotes Morgan that "AI interactions do not automatically compromise work product protections," and reminds the plaintiff that work product covers only material prepared "in anticipation of litigation or for trial."

Key quotations

  • “Notably, there is limited case law on this question, and the parties have not briefed it. Nonetheless, at this time and for this case only the Court adopts the reasoning set forth in Morgan v. V2X, Inc.” (at 2)
  • “However, if Plaintiff used generative AI to prepare his filings or for hearings in this matter, those interactions are shielded by the work product doctrine.” (at 2)

Limits

Expressly limited to this case and this time, on an unbriefed question. It adds a vote for Morgan, not new reasoning. The privilege-log requirement in the order concerns RFP 10 generally, not only AI material.

Later history

The parties reported a settlement in July 2026. An Oct. 6 order addressed post-settlement motions and required closing documents; it does not revisit the AI ruling.

Tate Group Automotive, LLC v. Legacy Automotive Capital, LLC

Mostly protected · Some pages produced
Tex. Bus. Ct. · June 3, 2026 · Dorfman, J.

After reading the chats in camera, a Texas Business Court judge held that most of a non-lawyer company principal's ChatGPT conversations were protected work product under Texas Rule 192.5, which covers material prepared "by or for a party." He agreed with Warner and Morgan, expressly disagreed with Heppner, ordered specific pages produced, and required the plaintiff to disclose every discovery document it had shared with ChatGPT.

Tate Group Automotive, LLC v. Legacy Automotive Capital, LLC, Cause No. 25-BC11B-0020 (Tex. Bus. Ct., 11th Div., June 3, 2026) (Dorfman, J.) (minute entry regarding ChatGPT materials in camera review).

Why it matters

The clearest rejection of Heppner by a court applying a state rule, and a represented company's non-lawyer principal (not a pro se litigant) got protection. It also shows the price: you may have to disclose what you uploaded.

Facts

Kris Tate, a non-lawyer principal of the plaintiff company, used ChatGPT about the dispute. The plaintiff withheld the conversations as attorney work product. Defendants argued that work product does not reach a non-lawyer's chats with an AI tool, or alternatively that using AI waived any protection, and asked the court to compel production and to order the plaintiff to identify all discovery materials shared with ChatGPT. The court reviewed the conversations in camera.

Holding

Specified pages of one document (#81, pp. 198–218, 260–283, 287–309, 311–331) must be produced because they are not protectable work product; the remainder may be withheld. The plaintiff must disclose to defendants, by Bates number, all discovery materials it shared with ChatGPT, including material produced under the protective order. The court will address any protective-order violation if one is shown, and recommended that the parties amend the protective order to make clear whether and how confidential information may be shared with AI tools.

Reasoning

The court agreed with Warner and Morgan that work product protection is "typically waived by disclosure to an adversary, or in circumstances that substantially increase the likelihood that an adversary will obtain the materials." It disagreed with Heppner, the defendants' main authority. And it noted that the federal cases do not control: Texas Rule 192.5(a)(1) protects material prepared or mental impressions developed in anticipation of litigation "by or for a party," which on its face reaches Tate's conversations. Following Morgan's tool-identification ruling, it held that work product "extends only so far" and required disclosure of what discovery went into ChatGPT.

Key quotations

  • “The short answer is that the Court agrees with the analysis and reasoning in the cases cited by Plaintiff: Warner v. Gilbarco, Inc. . . . and Morgan v. V2X, Inc.” (at 2)
  • “Further, the Court disagrees with the primary authority cited by Defendants in support of waiver, United States v. Heppner.” (at 2)
  • “Plaintiff must disclose to Defendants all discovery materials or products that it has shared with ChatGPT (by Bates number, if applicable), including any materials that were produced pursuant to the Protective Order.” (at 4)

Limits

A minute entry the court itself describes as "less formal" and "not intended to be final"; any party may seek a final ruling. Protection was page-specific after in camera review, not a blanket rule. The court did not discuss the provider's terms of service. Possible protective-order violations from uploading discovery were reserved.

Assini v. Hayward

Subpoena to OpenAI quashed
N.Y. Sup. Ct., Nassau Cnty. · June 4, 2026 · Fischer, J.New York

The first New York decision on the question. A Nassau County Supreme Court justice quashed a subpoena served on OpenAI for a pro se defendant's ChatGPT prompts and outputs. She expressly found Morgan v. V2X persuasive and declined to follow Heppner. The decision is short and adopts Morgan's reasoning rather than developing its own. It also referred the AI-using litigant to the new Part 161 rules on AI use in court submissions, with a warning about sanctions.

Assini v. Hayward, Index No. 607683/2024, NYSCEF Doc. No. 146 (Sup. Ct. Nassau County June 4, 2026) (Fischer, J.) (mot. seq. nos. 005–007), 2026 NY Slip Op 26086 (entered June 10, 2026).

Why it matters

The only New York decision so far. It sided with Morgan over Heppner under CPLR 3101(d), quashed a subpoena served directly on OpenAI, and in the same order warned the AI-using litigant to follow Part 161 or face sanctions.

Facts

Plaintiffs Fred Assini and Jason Napolitano sued, individually and derivatively on behalf of Alpha Tech Lending LLC. The defendants include John Recchio, who represents himself, and his entity Recchio Equity Capital LLC. Plaintiffs served a subpoena, then an amended subpoena, on non-party OpenAI OpCo, LLC for material from the accounts registered to Recchio. The amended subpoena sought two categories. The first was all prompts, inputs, uploaded materials and outputs used to draft filings, motions, sworn statements or communications sent to plaintiffs, their counsel or the court. The second was all prompts, queries, uploads and outputs referring to the plaintiffs, Alpha Tech, the claims or defenses, or filings in the action. Plaintiffs argued that the material bore on Recchio's factual assertions and was discoverable under CPLR 3101(a). Recchio moved to quash (mot. seq. 005), arguing that the materials were privileged "litigation-preparation." He also sought the same relief by order to show cause (mot. seq. 006). Plaintiffs cross-moved (mot. seq. 007) to have the amended subpoena so-ordered, to bar Recchio from further filings without leave, and for sanctions.

Holding

The court granted the motion to quash (005). Both subpoenas were quashed, and OpenAI need not produce records absent further court order. The order to show cause (006) was denied because Recchio filed no proof of service, and the court treated the service method an order to show cause prescribes as jurisdictional. The plaintiffs' cross-motion (007) was denied in full. The court also referred Recchio to 22 NYCRR Part 161 ("Use of Artificial Intelligence Technology"), directed him to be governed by it, and warned that failure to comply may result in sanctions.

Reasoning

The court set out the governing New York standards. CPLR 2304 allows a court to quash or condition a subpoena to a non-party. CPLR 3101(a) requires full disclosure of all matter material and necessary. The party asserting a privilege bears the burden of proving each element (Spectrum Systems). Materials prepared in anticipation of litigation carry a conditional privilege under CPLR 3101(d), which requires a showing that the material was prepared "solely" in anticipation of litigation and yields only to substantial need and undue hardship.

It then described the competing federal authority. Plaintiffs relied on Heppner, which the court summarized as holding that AI exchanges were not privileged, confidential or work product because the defendant created them on his own volition rather than at counsel's direction. The court contrasted Heppner with Morgan. It quoted Morgan's statements that a pro se litigant's AI use resembles the strategy-laden work Rule 26(b)(3) protects, that it is "entirely reasonable" to expect some privacy when using AI tools, and that public AI tools' data collection does not eliminate all privacy expectations or automatically waive protection. It noted that Morgan distinguished Heppner as a criminal case. The court then stated: "The Court finds the rationale presented in Morgan persuasive," and quashed the subpoenas. It did not separately apply the CPLR 3101(d) elements to the ChatGPT material, and it did not address relevance. Finally, it observed that while court rules do not prohibit AI, Recchio's use of AI "frustrates the litigation" (quoting Augustin v. Formula 3 Brook. Inc., Sup. Ct. Kings County 2025) and "cannot go unfettered." That observation led to the Part 161 referral.

Key quotations

  • “Materials prepared in anticipation of litigation are subject to a conditional privilege (CPLR § 3101[d]).” (slip op. at 3)
  • “given how AI tools function, it is entirely reasonable for a person to expect some privacy and confidentiality when interacting with these tools, even though they understand a third party is behind the tool collecting and storing their information.” (slip op. at 4 (quoting Morgan v. V2X, Inc.); this is Morgan's language, not Assini's own)
  • “The Court finds the rationale presented in Morgan persuasive.” (slip op. at 4)
  • “Accordingly, the Subpoena and Amended Subpoena shall be quashed.” (slip op. at 4)
  • “Notwithstanding same, defendant Recchio’s use of AI has not gone unnoticed by the Court.” (slip op. at 4)
  • “defendant Recchio is referred to 22 NYCRR §161, “Use of Artificial Intelligence Technology” and directed to be governed accordingly. Failure to abide by the Court Rules may result in sanctions.” (slip op. at 5)

Limits

A short trial-level order in a pro se case, not binding on other courts. It recites the CPLR 3101(d) standard, summarizes Heppner, quotes Morgan and finds Morgan persuasive; it never expressly finds the ChatGPT material was prepared “solely” in anticipation of litigation, and does not apply the elements. The “entirely reasonable … to expect some privacy” language is Morgan's, quoted. The subpoena was unusually broad (all prompts and outputs about the case); the ruling does not decide whether narrower requests aimed at specific factual statements would be quashed.

Later history

A notice of appeal was filed in July 2026; whether the appeal has been perfected is not verified. No later New York decision on AI privilege or work product has been identified.

Shealy v. Seaside Investments, LLC

Not work product · Production ordered
Mass. Super. Ct. (BLS) · June 2026 · Squires-Lee, J.

A Massachusetts business-court judge ordered production of ChatGPT material that a represented plaintiff's romantic partner generated at his request. Neither her queries nor the output were work product, because she was not his "representative" and counsel did not direct the work. The court distinguished Warner and Morgan as pro se cases and found the facts closer to Heppner.

Shealy v. Seaside Investments, LLC, No. 2684CV00799-BLS2 (Mass. Super. Ct., Suffolk Cnty., Bus. Litig. Sess. June 2026) (Squires-Lee, J.) (decision and order on defendants’ motion to compel; the signed opinion leaves the day blank).

Why it matters

The main counterweight to Morgan for represented parties: sending case documents to a spouse, partner or friend to "run through ChatGPT" is likely to produce discoverable material.

Facts

The case concerns a convertible promissory note and loan agreement tied to the sale of a business, with an expedited trial set for July 13, 2026. After the defendants sent a reservation-of-rights letter alleging default, Earl Wade Shealy, Jr. emailed the letter, the note and the purchase agreement to his romantic partner, Debbi Fields. She uploaded them to ChatGPT, which generated a response letter and other documents that she emailed back. She was directed by Shealy; no attorney was involved. Fields testified she was just "like a Xerox." Shealy produced the emails in redacted form and claimed work product protection.

Holding

Motion to compel allowed. Neither the queries of AI programs for legal assistance by a party's romantic partner nor the AI output are protected work product "unless performed at the direction of counsel." Nor is ChatGPT's output opinion work product. Plaintiffs had to produce the unredacted documents within five business days (and, separately, documents on Shealy's ability to prepay the note).

Reasoning

Massachusetts Rule 26(b)(3) protects material prepared by or for a party or its "representative," such as an attorney, consultant, surety, indemnitor, insurer or agent. Fields had "absolutely no substantive connection to the litigation or to Plaintiffs' counsel"; treating her as a representative would "expand the work product doctrine without meaningful limitation." ChatGPT was not a representative either. Warner and Morgan were inapposite because they "rely heavily on the fact that the parties were pro se"; here plaintiffs had "sophisticated, capable counsel" who "had nothing to do with" the AI use, which made the case "more akin" to Heppner. Finally, "AI is a tool not a person," so nothing it produces discloses an attorney's or representative's mental impressions.

Key quotations

  • “I conclude, as a matter of first impression, that neither the queries of AI programs for legal assistance by a party’s romantic partner nor the AI output are protected from disclosure under the work product doctrine unless performed at the direction of counsel.” (at 5)
  • “Both of those cases rely heavily on the fact that the parties were pro se and using publicly available AI programs to assist them in crafting legal strategy and preparing documents.” (at 5)
  • “This case differs significantly. Plaintiffs are represented by sophisticated, capable counsel.” (at 5)
  • “Nor does the output from ChatGPT constitute opinion work product. AI is a tool not a person.” (at 6)

Limits

The party did not use ChatGPT himself; a non-party did, at his request. So the case does not squarely hold that a represented party's own AI use is unprotected without counsel's direction. This is a failure of protection to attach, not a holding that sending protected work to a provider waives it. A trial-court ruling under the Massachusetts rule.

Later history

No later ruling on the AI issue has been identified.

Whisman v. Rajabi

No privilege · Work product protected, not waived
W.D. Ark. · July 15, 2026 · Ford, M.J.

A pro se plaintiff asked the court to declare her Microsoft Copilot conversations privileged. The court gave the same chats two different answers: no attorney-client privilege, because Copilot is not a lawyer, its terms allow Microsoft access and review, and it disclaims professional advice; but protected work product, because using the tool is not disclosure to an adversary. "AI programs are tools, not people."

Whisman v. Rajabi, No. 6:25-cv-06063-JTS, ECF No. 98 (W.D. Ark. July 15, 2026) (Ford, M.J.) (order on plaintiff’s motion to recognize and protect privileged communications with AI legal assistance tools).

Why it matters

The cleanest example of the privilege / work product split. Like Heppner, it uses the provider's terms to reject privilege; like Warner and Morgan, it holds that work product survives.

Facts

Veronica Whisman, representing herself, moved for an order declaring that her communications with AI legal assistance tools, including Microsoft Copilot, were attorney-client privileged or protected work product under Rule 26(b)(3).

Holding

Attorney-client privilege does not apply. The AI materials lack "at least two, if not three" elements: they are not communications with counsel, they are not fully confidential, and the platform is not designed to give professional advice. Work product does apply: her AI searches, conversations and document drafts are protected, and using Copilot did not waive that protection. The motion was granted.

Reasoning

On privilege, the court followed the Heppner structure. Copilot "is neither an attorney nor an employee of an attorney." Microsoft's privacy statement says it uses conversations to troubleshoot, prevent abuse and improve performance, that trained reviewers may read them, and it advises against sharing confidential data; so the conversations "are not completely confidential." The service agreement says AI services are not substitutes for professional advice. On work product, a pro se litigant may assert it (citing Warner). Waiver of work product "has to be a waiver to an adversary or in a way likely to get in an adversary's hand," and AI programs are "tools, not people": even with administrators in the background, the results "are unlikely to fall into the hands of the adversary."

Key quotations

  • “Applying these principles here, the AI searches and documents lack at least two, if not three, elements of the attorney-client privilege.” (at 2)
  • “As such, communications with Copilot, no matter how well intended, are not completely confidential and are accessible by Microsoft for certain purposes.” (at 2)
  • “Unlike the attorney-client privilege, the work-product waiver has to be a waiver to an adversary or in a way likely to get in an adversary’s hand.” (at 3)
  • “AI programs are tools, not people. Even if administrators are working in the background, the results of an individual’s search queries and/or conversations are unlikely to fall into the hands of the adversary.” (at 4)

Limits

The privilege ruling is about whether a person's own AI chats can become privileged, not about uploading a lawyer's existing advice. The court read Microsoft's terms as of July 13, 2026; it is not a finding about every Copilot product, account or setting.

Later history

On Sept. 4, 2026 the district judge granted summary judgment and dismissed the remaining claims; that order does not revisit the AI ruling.

Fry v. Fry

Sealing denied (redactions allowed)
E.D. Pa. · Aug. 27, 2026 · Marston, J.

In a Hague Convention case, a pro se father uploaded his documents to non-enterprise Claude before asking the court to seal them. After in camera review, Judge Marston refused wholesale sealing, reasoning in footnote 4 that material already given to a for-profit AI company under its consumer privacy and training terms is not private; she allowed targeted redactions. It is a sealing ruling, not a privilege ruling, but it treats consumer AI terms the way Heppner did.

Fry v. Fry, No. 2:26-cv-03469-KSM, ECF 61 (E.D. Pa. Aug. 27, 2026) (Marston, J.) (memorandum on renewed motion to seal)

Why it matters

Takes Heppner's consumer-terms logic from privilege to sealing: once documents go into a consumer AI account whose terms allow training, a court may treat them as no longer private. It also shows a court requiring an AI-use certification with a sealing motion.

Facts

Brett Kendall Fry filed a pro se petition under the Hague Convention and ICARA for the return of his children. He sought to seal or redact many exhibits. An earlier order required any renewed sealing motion to come with a one-page certification that either no AI tools were used or, if they were, which tools, every prompt and document input, and verification of every citation, with a wet signature. In his certification (Doc. 55-6), Fry disclosed that he had uploaded his redacted documents to "non-enterprise versions of Claude and Claude CoWork," Anthropic products, while preparing the motion.

Holding

After an in camera, document-by-document review, the court held that "a wholesale sealing order is not warranted" but that redactions in some documents were appropriate. It allowed redaction of personal identifiers (consistent with Fed. R. Civ. P. 5.2 and the local rule), names and contact information of nonparty military and medical personnel, and other categories listed in an appendix chart. In footnote 4, attached to the refusal of wholesale sealing, the court explained that full sealing of any document was inappropriate because the petitioner had uploaded the documents to consumer Claude.

Reasoning

The court applied the Third Circuit’s strong presumption of public access to judicial records (In re Avandia), which requires a document-by-document showing of a clearly defined and serious injury. On the AI point, footnote 4 reasons that documents uploaded to Claude "are processed on Anthropic’s servers, and Anthropic’s data privacy and data training pages make clear that such information is not private," citing Anthropic’s privacy policy and its model-training page, and United States v. Heppner, 820 F. Supp. 3d 292, 296 (S.D.N.Y. 2026). It concluded that "it would be inappropriate for the Court to seal a document that has already been provided to a private, for-profit research and artificial intelligence company."

Key quotations

  • “the Court finds that the full sealing of any document is not appropriate.” (slip op. at 5 n.4)
  • “Documents and information uploaded to Claude are processed on Anthropic’s servers, and Anthropic’s data privacy and data training pages make clear that such information is not private.” (slip op. at 5 n.4)
  • “it would be inappropriate for the Court to seal a document that has already been provided to a private, for-profit research and artificial intelligence company.” (slip op. at 5 n.4)

Limits

This is a footnote in a sealing ruling for a pro se litigant, not a privilege or waiver holding. The court still allowed targeted redactions; it rejected only wholesale sealing. The quoted Heppner page cite (296) is Fry’s.

The Hague petition was denied Aug. 14, 2026; a Third Circuit appeal was docketed Sept. 2. Fry moved for partial reconsideration (ECF 65, Aug. 31), and a later sealing motion was granted in part and denied in part on Oct. 1 (ECF 78–79).

Fontainebleau Florida Hotel, LLC v. Botach

Partly discoverable · Work product claims on a log
S.D. Fla. · Oct. 21, 2026 · Elfenbein, M.J.

In a defamation case, the hotel sought the defendant's AI chats by document request and by subpoenas to four AI providers. The court allowed chats about the hotel, its people and its counsel (relevant to punitive damages) but barred chats about the litigation, court orders and the judges. It rejected the defendant's blanket work-product objection but held that a represented party's own litigation preparation can be work product, claimed document by document on a privilege log. Using AI "does not automatically establish either discoverability or protection."

Fontainebleau Florida Hotel, LLC v. Botach, No. 1:25-cv-20251-JTK, ECF No. 289 (S.D. Fla. Oct. 21, 2026) (Elfenbein, M.J.) (second order after discovery hearing).

Why it matters

The newest decision, and the first to let a represented party claim work product for his own AI use without showing his lawyers directed it. It complicates the simple pro se / represented line.

Facts

After a liability ruling, the district court reopened discovery limited to damages. The hotel served a Seventh Request for Production and proposed Rule 45 subpoenas to four AI service providers, seeking the defendant's AI communications since December 2024 on seven topics: the hotel, the litigation, plaintiff's counsel, hotel employees and owners, court orders, the presiding judges, and deleted communications. The defendant objected on scope and work product grounds. The hotel also moved to compel him to consent to the provider subpoenas.

Holding

Requests about the hotel, plaintiff's counsel, and hotel employees, owners or representatives (and deleted chats on those subjects) are within damages discovery. Requests about "this litigation," court orders and the judges are not. The blanket work-product objection is overruled, but the defendant may withhold qualifying material on a Rule 26(b)(5)(A) privilege log. The motion to compel consent to the provider subpoenas is denied; the hotel may serve narrowed subpoenas, and the parties must agree how providers will produce so the defendant can screen for work product first.

Reasoning

The court separated relevance from protection. Chats about the hotel and its people may show intent and the persistence of the alleged conduct; chats about the case, orders or judges are too attenuated, and State Farm warns against punishing a defendant "for being an unsavory individual." On work product, Rule 26(b)(3)(A) "is not an attorney-exclusive protection." The court found Heppner persuasive only to the extent that AI chats "do not automatically become protected work product merely because counsel represents the user," and did not read it to make a represented party's own preparation categorically unprotected. Warner did not support a blanket objection either. Chats used to create public-facing statements "ordinarily would not" qualify.

Key quotations

  • “The Court emphasizes that a party’s communications with and use of AI does not automatically establish either discoverability or protection.” (at 21)
  • “At the same time, the Court does not read Heppner as establishing that all materials independently prepared by a represented party are categorically unprotected.” (at 19)
  • “Defendant may not withhold all communications within the permitted categories based on a generalized assertion that they concern litigation, and Plaintiff may not obtain genuinely protected litigation-preparation materials merely by describing them as AI communications.” (at 21)
  • “The Court further emphasizes that permitting the issuance of subpoenas does not establish their enforceability.” (at 23)

Limits

Permission to serve provider subpoenas is not a ruling enforcing them, and no provider production has been verified. Several requests failed on scope, not privilege. The court did not decide any provider-disclosure waiver theory.

Later history

An Oct. 28 order after a further hearing (ECF 303) set a midnight Oct. 30 deadline for the AI-related production "subject to any appeal." Reports indicate the district judge denied an emergency stay on Oct. 30; the actual production and work-product log have not been verified. The defendant's continued deposition was set for Nov. 4.

Tremblay v. OpenAI, Inc.

Counsel's testing protected · No broad waiver
N.D. Cal. · Aug. 8, 2024 · Martínez-Olguín, J.

Authors suing OpenAI attached ChatGPT prompts and outputs to their complaint. A magistrate judge ordered them to produce all their pre-suit testing, including the failed attempts. The district judge reversed: counsel's prompts are opinion work product, and putting some results in the complaint did not waive protection for the rest.

Tremblay v. OpenAI, Inc., No. 3:23-cv-03223-AMO, ECF No. 167 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 8, 2024).

Why it matters

The starting point for the "testing" line: you can protect your investigation, but what you put in front of the court must be testable.

Facts

Plaintiffs' counsel tested ChatGPT before filing and attached favorable prompts and outputs (Exhibit B) to the complaint. OpenAI sought the account information of the testers and all pre-suit prompts and outputs, including ones that did not support the claims. Plaintiffs offered to produce the full threads behind the Exhibit B examples but refused the rest. Magistrate Judge Illman compelled everything, treating the material as fact work product subject to broad subject-matter waiver.

Holding

Plaintiffs' motion for relief from the magistrate judge's order was granted. The prompts were queries crafted by counsel and are opinion work product; the order applying broad subject-matter waiver to them was contrary to law.

Reasoning

Opinion work product is "virtually undiscoverable." It can be reached by waiver only where counsel's mental impressions are at issue and the need is compelling, and any waiver must be "closely tailored" to fairness. Neither the order nor OpenAI showed that counsel's mental impressions were at issue. Treating counsel's testing as "bare facts" misapplied the law.

Key quotations

  • “This is a misapplication of law as the ChatGPT prompts were queries crafted by counsel and contain counsel’s mental impressions and opinions about how to interrogate ChatGPT, in an effort to vindicate Plaintiffs’ copyrights against the alleged infringements.” (at 3)

Limits

Decided before Heppner and about a very different use: lawyers testing the adversary's product, not a client consulting a chatbot. It does not decide whether sending material to an AI provider waives privilege.

Prompts and drafts protected · AI case summaries produced
D. Nev. · Sept. 29, 2026 · Traum, J.

This is the leading case on discovery of an expert's ChatGPT history. A magistrate judge ordered production of a testifying expert's entire ChatGPT log. The district judge held that order clearly erroneous and split the log. ChatGPT's case-law summaries are "facts or data considered" and must be produced. The expert's own prompts, a ChatGPT-generated deposition outline and ChatGPT-assisted draft-report material are protected. Using ChatGPT waived nothing, because ChatGPT is "not a person."

Villanueva v. Las Vegas Metro. Police Dep't, No. 2:24-cv-00125-ART-DJA, ECF No. 112 (D. Nev. Sept. 29, 2026) (Traum, J.) (ruling on objections to Magistrate Judge Albregts's Dec. 23, 2025 bench rulings).

Why it matters

What the AI tells your expert can be discoverable; what the expert asks it is protected. AI research summaries an expert considered must be produced, which exposes any hallucinated citations to cross-examination.

Facts

Jose Villanueva sued Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department officer Francis Soriano and the Department under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and state law. The claims arise from events while he was detained at the Clark County Detention Center. His expert on excessive force, Tom Melton, testified at his October 1, 2025 deposition that he used ChatGPT to research and draft his expert report, and that he sometimes copied and pasted ChatGPT output into the report. Plaintiff had not disclosed the AI use in response to an earlier subpoena for all data and information the expert considered. After the deposition, plaintiff refused to produce the ChatGPT history log, listing it as "Attorney/Expert Work Product; Trial Preparation: Materials." Defendants moved to compel (ECF 44). On December 23, 2025, Magistrate Judge Albregts granted the motion from the bench. He held that the entire log should have been disclosed under Rule 26(a)(2)(B)(ii) and (iii) because Melton considered it, that it was not a protected draft report, and that the attorney-client privilege did not apply. He did not review the roughly 40-page log in camera. Instead he relied on plaintiff's description of it in three categories: case summaries of excessive-force law, a deposition outline Melton used to predict questions, and prompts to analyze and draft portions of the report, such as critiques of the opposing expert. Plaintiff objected (ECF 70).

Holding

Judge Traum held that ordering production of the entire log was clearly erroneous or contrary to law. She granted the objection in part and overruled it in part. Plaintiff must produce the ChatGPT responses that are case-law summaries, because they are "facts or data" the expert considered. Three categories are protected and need not be produced: (1) Melton's questions and prompts to ChatGPT, (2) the portions used to create a deposition outline, and (3) the portions used to create draft-report material. Melton's use of ChatGPT did not waive any privilege. The court found no clear error in the magistrate judge's decision not to review the log in camera, and noted that it had itself reviewed the log in camera.

Reasoning

The court started from the 2010 amendments to Rule 26. Experts must disclose the "facts or data considered," read broadly to include anything with factual ingredients that the expert considered, not only what the expert relied on (Republic of Ecuador v. Mackay, 9th Cir.). Draft reports are protected "regardless of the form in which the draft is recorded" (Rule 26(b)(4)(B)). Applying Hirata, a District of Nevada decision holding that summaries an expert consulted must be disclosed, the court held that ChatGPT's case summaries were considered and must be produced. It noted defendants' point that the log is the only place they can test alleged hallucinated citations and quotations the expert relied on or considered.

The prompts were treated differently. A question is not something an expert can "consider" as facts or data, just as an expert does not consider his own online search queries. The prompts would also reveal the expert's mental impressions and theory of the case, so they are protected whether viewed as part of a draft report under Rule 26(b)(4)(B) or as trial-preparation material under Rule 26(b)(3). The deposition-outline material is not facts or data, because Melton used it to prepare for testimony, not to form opinions. It would also reveal his focus and priorities, so Rule 26(b)(3)(B) bars its production. The draft-report material is protected as a draft because Melton copied output directly into his report. Alternatively, it reveals his mental impressions. On waiver, the court held that because ChatGPT "is not a person" but an application used for research, drafting and editing, any privileged information was not waived by using it. Finally, the court said that defendants' concerns about the expert's methodology and reliance on ChatGPT can be addressed at trial through impeachment.

Key quotations

  • “The legal case summaries generated by ChatGPT constitute “facts or data” that Melton considered and must be disclosed under Rule 26.” (slip op. at 6)
  • “As Defendant correctly contends, the history log is the only place where Defendant can review alleged “hallucinated case citations…quotes…and references to broad legal standards” that Melton relied upon, or at least considered, to reach his opinion.” (slip op. at 7)
  • “The Court finds that a question is not something that an expert can “consider” or “rely” upon as “facts or data” in reaching their opinion, just as an expert would not “consider” or “rely” upon their online research queries.” (slip op. at 7)
  • “Rule 26(b)(4)(B) mandates the protection of expert drafts “regardless of the form in which the draft is recorded.”” (slip op. at 9)
  • “Because ChatGPT is not a person, but was an application used in this instance as a research, drafting, and editing program, the Court finds that any privileged information is not waived by Melton’s use of the program.” (slip op. at 10)
  • “The Court also notes that many of Defendant’s concerns about Melton’s methodology and reliance on ChatGPT are appropriate to address at trial as matters of impeachment.” (slip op. at 10–11)

Limits

The district judge sustained the objection only in part. Do not say the court protected the whole log, and do not say it ordered the whole log produced. That was the magistrate judge's order, which was partly reversed. The ruling applies the specific federal expert-disclosure rules (Rule 26(a)(2)(B) and 26(b)(4)), so it does not decide whether a party's or lawyer's own AI use is privileged. The court applied Rule 26(b)(3)(B)'s protection for the "mental impressions" of a party's "representative" to a testifying expert's prompts and outline. That is an aggressive reading that other courts may not share. The "not a person" waiver sentence is brief and does not discuss OpenAI's terms. The ruling is subject to a pending reconsideration motion (see later history).

Later history

Defendants moved for reconsideration of ECF 112 on Oct. 20, 2026 (ECF 113); responses are due Nov. 3. The motion's contents have not been reviewed, so it is not confirmed that it targets the ChatGPT ruling.

Dunn v. LexisNexis Risk Solutions, Inc.

Criteria, not per-tool consent
D. Colo. · Aug. 31, 2026 · Dominguez Braswell, M.J.

Magistrate Judge Dominguez Braswell, who also decided Morgan v. V2X, entered the parties' agreed AI provision but struck one disputed sentence. That sentence would have required disclosure, consent, and amendment of the order every time a party wanted to use a new AI tool. She held that once the parties have agreed on tool-agnostic criteria (no training, no third-party disclosure beyond what service delivery requires, and the ability to delete), competent counsel can judge new tools themselves. Along the way she rejected the claim that AI risk is "identical" to cloud storage, and found it reasonable to keep the other side's confidential information out of consumer-grade tools.

Dunn v. LexisNexis Risk Sols., Inc., No. 1:26-cv-01249-GPG-MDB, ECF No. 32 (D. Colo. Aug. 31, 2026) (Dominguez Braswell, M.J.)

Why it matters

The best judicial authority for tool-agnostic criteria over approved lists, and the clearest explanation of why consumer and enterprise tools can be treated differently when the other side's data is at stake.

Facts

Colin Dunn sued LexisNexis Risk Solutions, Inc. and Farmers Insurance Exchange in the District of Colorado. The parties first filed a protective-order motion with competing AI provisions (Doc. 24). At a hearing, the court offered "limited guidance" and ordered them to confer again. They then agreed on nearly all of an AI provision and filed a second joint motion (Doc. 29, proposed order Doc. 29-1). As the court describes it, the agreed provision bars uploading confidential information to certain AI platforms. It permits secure platforms that limit training and disclosure and allow deletion, and it identifies the tools the parties intend to use. One sentence at the end remained in dispute. It would have required disclosure, consent, and amendment each time a party sought to use a new AI tool with confidential information. Defendants supported it and plaintiff objected.

Holding

The court granted the second joint motion and entered the proposed order without the disputed sentence. The agreed AI provision stays in; the per-tool consent-and-amend requirement comes out. As a result, a party may adopt a new AI tool without going back to the court or getting the other side's consent, as long as the tool meets the agreed standing requirements: no model training on inputs, no third-party disclosure beyond what is essential for service delivery, and the ability to delete information. The order describes the agreed paragraph (Doc. 29-1 at 11–12) rather than quoting it.

Reasoning

The court first rejected two of plaintiff's framings. It did not accept that defendants were acting from a "perverse incentive," calling knowledge of how confidential information is handled "a legitimate interest." It also rejected plaintiff's claim that AI risks are "identical" to cloud storage. Morgan had compared AI to email for privacy purposes, but cloud and email systems operate under "more mature frameworks." AI is newer and processes inputs in ways that are not fully transparent, so it presents greater risk. The court then distinguished a party's own data, where the party accepts the risk, from another party's data, where the producer cannot manage the risk without an agreement or court order. On that basis it was reasonable to keep confidential information out of consumer-grade tools that lack enterprise assurances. Footnote 3 acknowledges Craig Ball's critique ("The AI Protective Order Double Standard") that the consumer/enterprise line may burden people who cannot afford enterprise access. The court says it takes the concern seriously and had raised it in Morgan, but must weigh the competing interests case by case. Finally, the disputed sentence was more than transparency. It would create a consent-and-amend process requiring motion practice for every new tool, and it was unnecessary because both sides have competent counsel who can apply the agreed criteria.

Key quotations

  • “The Court rejects the notion that the risks are identical.” (at 2)
  • “Thus, when it comes to uploading confidential information, AI systems present greater risk due to their opacity and continued evolution.” (at 3)
  • “Given the pace of change in consumer-grade AI, and the limited visibility into how these tools process data, it is reasonable for a party to request that confidential information be kept out of consumer-grade AI tools that do not offer the same assurances and configurations found in certain enterprise offerings.” (at 3)
  • “It is a consent-and-amend process that will require motion practice every time a party adopts a new tool.” (at 4)
  • “By exchanging information about their respective tools, the parties were able to understand each other’s concerns and agree on an AI provision that sets clear, tool-agnostic requirements: no model training on inputs, no third-party disclosure beyond what is essential for service delivery, and the ability to delete information.” (at 4–5)

Limits

This is a magistrate judge's ruling on one disputed sentence. The AI provision itself was agreed, not imposed. The court's description of the agreed terms is a summary, so quote the entered order for exact wording. The parties did disclose their tools to each other once, at the court's urging. The court rejected only the continuing consent-and-amend requirement, not transparency. The order does not describe the underlying claims.

Later history

The order states that the court "will enter" the proposed protective order (Doc. 29-1) without the disputed language. The entered protective order is a separate document.

United States v. Sarris

Consumer vs. enterprise terms (criminal)
S.D.N.Y. · Sept. 30, 2026 · Marrero, J.

Sarris is a consent protective order in an S.D.N.Y. criminal case whose ¶ 9 governs the defense's use of AI on government discovery. Instead of banning "publicly accessible" tools by brand name, it draws the line at consumer versus commercial or enterprise terms of service. It lets the defense use a tool once counsel has taken reasonable steps to confirm four safeguards, and it excludes local software and ordinary embedded features from the definition of "AI tool." It binds only the defense.

United States v. Sarris, No. 26 Cr. 399 (VM), ECF No. 13 (S.D.N.Y. Sept. 30, 2026) (Marrero, J.)

Why it matters

An entered criminal protective order that draws the line by the terms governing the account rather than by brand, asks counsel for reasonable diligence rather than guarantees, and carves out local software and ordinary embedded features.

Facts

Sarris is a criminal prosecution before Judge Victor Marrero. The order recites that it was entered "[u]pon the application of the United States of America, with the consent of the undersigned counsel," after the defendant requested Rule 16 discovery. Three AUSAs signed for the government on September 25, 2026. Timothy J. Treanor and Arlo Devlin-Brown signed as counsel for William Sarris. Judge Marrero so-ordered it on September 30, 2026. Apart from the AI paragraph, the order follows the familiar S.D.N.Y. structure. It defines Disclosure Material, Sealed Material, Attorney's Possession Only (APO) and Attorney's Eyes Only (AEO) material. It limits use to defending the case and requires return or destruction within 30 days after the appeal period (¶ 14). The court keeps jurisdiction after the case ends (¶ 15). Paragraph 9, "Restrictions on Use of Artificial Intelligence Tools to Process Disclosure Materials," is the AI provision. The text reviewed is an OCR of the scanned order.

Holding

Paragraph 9 has three parts. (a) Definition. An "AI tool" is any automated system that uses machine learning or similar techniques to process inputs and generate outputs, recommendations, or predictions. That includes large language models, generative AI services, and AI-assisted software, whether cloud-based or not. Two things are excluded. One is (i) software that runs entirely on systems the defense controls, so that no Disclosure Material goes to any third party. The other is (ii) standard office, communication, legal-research, or litigation-support software that would otherwise count only because of embedded assistive features: spelling or grammar correction, text completion, search, OCR, or document ranking and classification. That exclusion also applies only if no Disclosure Material is sent to a third party. (b) Prohibitions. The defense team may not submit Disclosure Materials to a "consumer (i.e., non-commercial)" version of an AI tool. The order defines that as a version offered to individuals under consumer terms of service, rather than to organizations under commercial or enterprise terms. Nor may the defense use any AI tool (examples: OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Google) "under terms or account settings" that permit model training on submitted data, public access, disclosure to third parties, or, "except as specified below," retention. Retention is therefore not banned outright; the exception points to ¶ 9(c). (c) Permission. The defense may use an AI tool if defense counsel "has taken reasonable steps to confirm" four things. The tool (i) maintains the confidentiality of the materials. (ii) It will not expose them to third parties not authorized under the order "except as required by law." (iii) It will not retain or use them for model training. (iv) It permits deletion of submitted data. At the end of the case, the defense will delete the materials from its accounts with the tool "or request their deletion," consistent with ¶ 14. Nothing in ¶ 9 or elsewhere in the order limits the government's use of AI on defense materials.

Reasoning

As a consent order, Sarris contains no judicial reasoning. The only stated rationale is the order's own sentence in ¶ 9(b): such tools "pose unique risks to the security and integrity of Disclosure Materials, given the practical inability to claw back or delete data once it has been incorporated into a model." Structurally, ¶ 9 pairs a ban based on the type of terms (consumer terms) with a ban based on criteria (training, public access, third-party disclosure, retention). It then adds a safe harbor keyed to counsel's reasonable diligence rather than a list of approved products. The entered text differs from the "publicly accessible" wording in Dickens and Ipus-Barrios in six ways:

(1) It defines the prohibited category by terms of service instead of listing consumer products by name.

(2) It looks at the "terms or account settings" of the account actually used, rather than at whether the vendor generally "uses submitted data" for training.

(3) It replaces flat conditions the tool must meet with "reasonable steps to confirm" by defense counsel.

(4) It adds an "except as required by law" exception to the third-party-exposure condition.

(5) It requires that the tool permit deletion and lets the defense delete or request deletion at the end, rather than requiring that all materials "will be removed."

(6) It carves local software and ordinary embedded features out of the definition.

Key quotations

  • ““AI tool” does not include (i) software that operates entirely on systems controlled by the defense, such that no Disclosure Materials are transmitted to any third party; or (ii) standard office, communication, legal-research, or litigation-support software by reason of embedded assistive features such as spelling or grammar correction, text completion, search, optical character recognition, or document ranking and classification, provided that no Disclosure Material is transmitted to any third party.” (¶ 9(a))
  • “The defense team will not submit Disclosure Materials to a consumer (i.e., non-commercial) version of an AI tool, meaning a version offered to individuals under consumer terms of service rather than to organizations under commercial or enterprise terms.” (¶ 9(b))
  • “The defense team will not submit Disclosure Materials to an AI tool (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Google) under terms or account settings that permit use of submitted data for its own model training, allows submitted data to be publicly accessed, discloses submitted data to third parties, or, except as specified below, retains submitted data.” (¶ 9(b))
  • “The defense may submit Disclosure Materials to an AI tool provided that defense counsel has taken reasonable steps to confirm that (i) the AI tool maintains the confidentiality of Disclosure Materials; (ii) the AI tool will not expose any Disclosure Materials to third parties not authorized under this Protective Order to receive such materials except as required by law; (iii) the AI tool will not retain or use any Disclosure Materials for model training; and (iv) the AI tool permits deletion of submitted data.” (¶ 9(c))
  • “At the conclusion of this case, the defense will delete Disclosure Materials from its accounts with any such AI tool or request their deletion, consistent with paragraph 14.” (¶ 9(c))

Limits

A consent order, not a ruling; no precedential weight. Under its own definition the test is whether a plan is offered to individuals under consumer terms, so "paid" does not equal "commercial"; check each vendor's current terms. Exclusion (ii) applies only if no Disclosure Material goes to a third party, so a cloud assistant built into office software must satisfy ¶ 9(c). It does not restrict the government.

Later history

None reflected in the materials reviewed.

ANR Pipeline Co. v. B&B Metal Processing Co.

Judge adds AI clause on his own
E.D. Wis. · Oct. 2, 2026 · Stadtmueller, J.

Judge Stadtmueller granted the parties' joint motion for a protective order in a commercial case but added, as one of his own modifications, a new ¶ F on AI. It permits a receiving party to use AI on discovery materials only in a secure environment under enforceable contract terms that bar the provider from retaining, using, disclosing, selling, or training on the materials, bar public or third-party access, and require commercially reasonable safeguards. It also flatly bars entering discovery materials into any publicly available or consumer-based AI platform.

ANR Pipeline Co., LLC v. B&B Metal Processing Co., No. 2:26-cv-01366-JPS, ECF No. 19 (E.D. Wis. Oct. 2, 2026) (Stadtmueller, J.)

Why it matters

A judge writing AI terms into an agreed protective order as a default, in an ordinary commercial case, with a flat bar on consumer platforms.

Facts

ANR Pipeline Company sued B&B Metal Processing Co., Eldorados Land Acquisitions LLC, and two individual defendants in the Eastern District of Wisconsin. On September 22, 2026, the parties filed a joint motion with a stipulated proposed protective order (ECF 17, 17-1). They reported that the case would involve disclosure of confidential technical information about the parties. On October 2, 2026, the court found good cause and granted the motion. It entered the order with what it called "four minor modifications": clarifying restricted versus sealed filings, letting the public challenge designations, letting attorneys keep work product, and, fourth, adding ¶ F on AI. The order presents ¶ F as the court's own addition. It does not say whether the parties' draft addressed AI at all.

Holding

Paragraph F lets a receiving party use AI tools "in connection with discovery materials produced in this action" only if two conditions are met. First, the tool is operated in a secure environment. Second, it is subject to enforceable contractual terms that (a) prohibit the provider from retaining, using, disclosing, selling, or training on the materials; (b) prohibit the materials from being made publicly available or accessible to any other customer or third party; and (c) require commercially reasonable administrative, technical, and physical safeguards. Separately, discovery materials may not be entered into "any publicly available or consumer-based AI platform." All AI use must remain consistent with the order and with the rule that discovery is used only for this litigation. The paragraph binds every receiving party equally. It has no AI-specific deletion requirement, and no notice or approval process for particular tools.

Reasoning

The court gave no reasons specific to AI. Its analysis addresses only the general Rule 26(c) standard under Seventh Circuit law. Protective orders are an exception to public discovery, and parties must show good cause even when they agree. A blanket order is acceptable if designations are made in good faith and the parties and public can challenge them (citing Citizens First and County Materials). The court then listed its modifications, calling them "minor," and pointed to ¶ F for the AI terms. The clause therefore reads as a judge-imposed default, not the outcome of a dispute between the parties.

Key quotations

  • “(4) to establish parameters for the use of Artificial Intelligence as it pertains to confidential documents” (at 3)
  • “A receiving party may use AI tools in connection with discovery materials produced in this action only if the AI tool is operated in a secure environment and is subject to enforceable contractual terms that (a) prohibit the provider from retaining, using, disclosing, selling, or using the materials to train, improve, or develop any AI system; (b) prohibit the materials from being made publicly available or accessible to any other customer or third party; and (c) require commercially reasonable administrative, technical, and physical safeguards to protect the confidentiality of the materials.” (¶ F, at 9)
  • “Discovery materials, whether designated CONFIDENTIAL or ATTORNEYS’ EYES ONLY, shall not be entered into any publicly available or consumer-based AI platform.” (¶ F, at 9–10)
  • “Any use of AI tools must remain consistent with this Order and with the requirement that discovery materials be used solely for purposes of this litigation.” (¶ F, at 10)

Limits

The order gives no reasons, so it shows judicial practice, not persuasive analysis. There is a scope tension. The introduction describes ¶ F as concerning "confidential documents," but ¶ F's first sentence covers all "discovery materials produced in this action," designated or not. "Secure environment," "consumer-based," and "retaining" are undefined. The order does not address whether brief processing or storage by the provider counts as retention.

United States v. Dickens

Reciprocal: binds the government too
S.D.N.Y. · Oct. 5, 2026 · Schofield, J.

Dickens is an S.D.N.Y. consent protective order that uses the "publicly accessible" AI formulation with a list of brand-name examples. Unlike most such orders, it also binds the government. The government may not submit "Defense Materials" to publicly accessible tools or to tools that train on, expose, disclose, or retain data, and it must remove them at the end of the case. It is the example to use for reciprocity.

United States v. Dickens, No. 23 Cr. 14 (LGS), ECF No. 37 (S.D.N.Y. Oct. 5, 2026) (Schofield, J.) (filed Oct. 6, 2026)

Why it matters

Defense lawyers produce sensitive material too. Dickens shows S.D.N.Y. judges entering orders that limit the government's AI use of defense materials. It also shows the weakness of brand lists: every named product has enterprise versions. At least seven S.D.N.Y. orders with government-side limits had been entered by late October, the earliest in United States v. Thomas, 26 Cr. 233 (Swain, J.) (Sept. 8, 2026).

Facts

The order was entered "upon the application of the United States of America, with the consent of the undersigned counsel." It recites that discovery was requested under Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.1(b)(2)(B), the disclosure provision for revocation hearings. That suggests a supervised-release or probation violation proceeding in a 2023 case rather than a new prosecution, but the order does not say so. A Special Assistant U.S. Attorney and defense counsel signed on October 2, 2026, and the court signed on October 5. Besides the government's "Disclosure Material," ¶ 1 defines "Defense Materials": materials the defense discloses under Rules 16, 26.2, or 32.1(b)(2)(B) or other defense disclosure obligations. Paragraph 7 is the AI provision.

Holding

Paragraph 7(a) defines "AI tool" broadly, with no carve-outs for local software or embedded features. On the defense side, ¶ 7(b) bars the defense team from submitting Disclosure Materials to "a publicly accessible version of an AI tool," naming eight examples (ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity, Midjourney, Canva). It also bars any tool whose provider or vendor (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Google) trains on submitted data, allows public access, discloses data to third parties, or, "except as specified below," retains it. Under ¶ 7(c), the defense may use a tool that maintains confidentiality, will not expose materials to unauthorized third parties, and will not retain or use them for training, and all materials must be "removed from the AI tool(s) at the conclusion of this case." On the government side, ¶¶ 7(d)–(e) impose the same restrictions on the government's handling of Defense Materials, with the same eight examples and the same removal requirement.

Reasoning

As a consent order, Dickens contains no judicial reasoning. The only stated rationale is in ¶ 7(b): "Publicly accessible AI tools pose unique risks to the security and integrity of Disclosure Materials, given the practical inability to claw back or delete data once it has been incorporated into a model." The government-side paragraphs repeat the operative rules but not that sentence. The conditions for permitted use are framed as facts about the tool that must be true. There is no "reasonable steps" standard for counsel.

Key quotations

  • “The defense team will not submit Disclosure Materials to a publicly accessible version of an AI tool (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity, Midjourney, Canva).” (¶ 7(b))
  • “The defense may submit Disclosure Materials to an AI tool (i) that maintains the confidentiality of Disclosure Materials; (ii) that will not expose any Disclosure Materials to third parties not authorized under this Protective Order to receive such materials; (iii) that will not retain or use any Disclosure Materials for model training; and (iv) all Disclosure Materials will be removed from the AI tool(s) at the conclusion of this case.” (¶ 7(c))
  • “The Government will not submit Defense Materials to a publicly accessible version of an AI tool (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity, Midjourney, Canva).” (¶ 7(d))
  • “The Government will not submit Defense Materials to an AI tool for which the AI tool provider or vendor (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Google) uses submitted data for its own model training, allows submitted data to be publicly accessed, discloses submitted data to third parties, or, except as specified below, retains submitted data.” (¶ 7(d))

Limits

Paragraph 7(e)(iii) refers to "Defense Sealed Materials," a term the order never defines, which looks like a drafting slip. The order says materials will be "removed," not "deleted." It names eight tools. It is a consent order with no precedential weight.

United States v. Ipus-Barrios

Standard "publicly accessible" form
S.D.N.Y. · Oct. 9, 2026 · Engelmayer, J.

Ipus-Barrios is a standard S.D.N.Y. consent protective order using the "publicly accessible" formulation. It bars the defense from submitting discovery to publicly accessible AI tools (listed by brand) or to tools whose vendor trains on, exposes, discloses, or retains data, and it requires removal at the end of the case. It binds only the defense, and it is a clean baseline to set beside Sarris.

United States v. Ipus-Barrios, No. 26 Cr. 310 (PAE), ECF No. 18 (S.D.N.Y. Oct. 9, 2026) (Engelmayer, J.)

Why it matters

The standard S.D.N.Y. form, still being entered after Sarris. Set beside Sarris it makes the drafting choices visible: brand list vs. terms of service, vendor-level vs. account-level test, flat conditions vs. reasonable steps, "removed" vs. delete or request deletion.

Facts

This is a criminal prosecution in which the defendant requested Rule 16 discovery. The order was entered on the government's application with the consent of defense counsel. The AUSAs signed on October 7, 2026 and defense counsel on October 8, and Judge Engelmayer so-ordered it on October 9. It has Disclosure Material, Sealed Material, and APO categories, but no AEO tier. A separate ¶ 13 bars giving Disclosure Material to foreign persons or entities except defense personnel or with court authorization. Paragraph 7 is the AI provision.

Holding

Paragraph 7(a) defines "AI tool" broadly, with no exclusions. Paragraph 7(b) bars the defense team from submitting Disclosure Materials to "a publicly accessible version of an AI tool," with the same eight brand examples as Dickens. It also bars any tool whose provider or vendor (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Google) uses submitted data for model training, allows public access, discloses data to third parties, or, except as specified below, retains it. Paragraph 7(c) permits a tool that maintains confidentiality, will not expose materials to unauthorized third parties, and will not retain or use them for training, provided all materials are removed at the end of the case. There is no restriction on the government.

Reasoning

As a consent order, it contains no reasoning. Unlike Dickens, Sarris, and Wilkes, ¶ 7(b) does not include the sentence saying such tools "pose unique risks" because data cannot be clawed back. The ¶ 7(c) conditions are framed as facts about the tool, not as diligence by counsel. The "except as specified below" retention exception has no express counterpart below other than the end-of-case removal requirement. That suggests retention during the case is tolerated so long as the materials are removed at the end.

Key quotations

  • “The defense team will not submit Disclosure Materials to a publicly accessible version of an AI tool (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity, Midjourney, Canva).” (¶ 7(b))
  • “The defense team will not submit Disclosure Materials to an AI tool for which the AI tool provider or vendor (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Google) uses submitted data for its own model training, allows submitted data to be publicly accessed, discloses submitted data to third parties, or, except as specified below, retains submitted data.” (¶ 7(b))
  • “The defense may submit Disclosure Materials to an AI tool (i) that maintains the confidentiality of Disclosure Materials; (ii) that will not expose any Disclosure Materials to third parties not authorized under this Protective Order to receive such materials; (iii) that will not retain or use any Disclosure Materials for model training; and (iv) all Disclosure Materials will be removed from the AI tool(s) at the conclusion of this case.” (¶ 7(c))

Limits

This is a consent order with no precedential weight. The brand list is ambiguous because each named product also exists in business or enterprise versions. The order does not say what "publicly accessible" means.

Mount Sinai Hospital v. CVS Health Corp.

Parties agree AI work is work product
S.D.N.Y. · Oct. 13, 2026 · Figueredo, M.J.

Mount Sinai is an agreed S.D.N.Y. protective order in a hospital-versus-CVS commercial dispute. Besides barring designated material from publicly accessible or training AI platforms, it declares that AI outputs derived from designated information "will be deemed attorney work product," along with prompts, inputs, logs, settings, and similar materials. It is the parties' agreement to treat AI work as work product, entered by the court, not a judicial ruling that it is.

Mount Sinai Hosp. v. CVS Health Corp., No. 1:26-cv-04143-GHW-VF, ECF No. 60 (S.D.N.Y. Oct. 13, 2026) (Figueredo, M.J.)

Why it matters

Represented parties can agree in advance that their AI prompts and outputs are work product, avoiding the Heppner / Shealy fight later.

Facts

Mount Sinai Hospital, Mount Sinai Beth Israel, and St. Luke's-Roosevelt Hospital sued CVS Health Corporation, Caremark entities, and WellPartner. The case is assigned to Judge Woods, with Magistrate Judge Figueredo. The order recites that the parties "jointly request entry" of a protective order. It explains that discovery in this "commercial dispute between a hospital and several healthcare companies" will include claim-level prescription and reimbursement data, PBM pricing and reimbursement methods, 340B remittance information, and protected health information. Magistrate Judge Figueredo entered it on October 13, 2026. The AI provision is ¶ 7(e), within ¶ 7 ("Use and Disclosure of Protected Material").

Holding

Paragraph 7(e) has two parts. First, a party receiving Confidential or Highly Confidential–AEO documents may not load or transfer them to a "publicly accessible" large language model or AI platform. Nor may it use a non-public platform where the documents "may be used to train public models or otherwise made accessible" to unauthorized users. Second, "for avoidance of doubt," any output of a "permissible generative artificial intelligence tool" that contains, reflects, incorporates, or is derived from designated information "will be deemed attorney work product." The work-product protections include prompts, queries, instructions, inputs, outputs, logs, model or tool settings, and similar materials created by or for a party, its counsel, a litigation-support vendor or consultant, or an expert in connection with the action. The paragraph does not limit expert disclosures under Rule 26(a)(2) or the court's power to order disclosure for good cause. There is no AI-specific deletion requirement and no contract-terms test.

Reasoning

The order gives no reasoning; it rests on the parties' agreement. Paragraphs 14 and 20 say the order is entered on the parties' representations and agreement to facilitate discovery. Paragraph 20 adds that entry "is not a judicial determination that any particular material qualifies for protection under Rule 26(c) or otherwise." Structurally, ¶ 7(e) pairs a restriction on risky platforms with a mutual agreement that each side's permitted AI work is work product. Expert-disclosure rules and the court's good-cause power remain as limits.

Key quotations

  • “Any party that receives designated Confidential or Highly Confidential – Attorneys’ Eyes Only documents shall not load, import, submit, or otherwise transfer the designated Confidential or Highly Confidential – Attorneys’ Eyes Only documents to a publicly accessible Large Language Model (“LLM”) or Artificial Intelligence (“AI”) platform, nor may any party utilize any non-public LLM or AI platforms where the designated Confidential or Highly Confidential – Attorneys’ Eyes Only documents may be used to train public models or otherwise made accessible to other users of the LLM or AI platform not authorized to receive such materials.” (¶ 7(e), at 9–10)
  • “For avoidance of doubt, any output or result generated by a permissible generative artificial intelligence tool that contains, reflects, incorporates, or is derived from Confidential or Highly Confidential – Attorneys’ Eyes Only Information will be deemed attorney work product.” (¶ 7(e), at 10)
  • “The attorney-work-product protections set forth in this Paragraph include any prompts, queries, instructions, inputs, outputs, logs, model or tool settings, and similar materials created by or for a Party, its counsel, any litigation support vendor or consultant, or any Expert in connection with this Action, including such materials used in connection with the work of any Expert.” (¶ 7(e), at 10)
  • “Nothing in this Paragraph limits any disclosure required by Rule 26(a)(2) or the Court’s authority to order disclosure for good cause.” (¶ 7(e), at 10)
  • “Entry of this Order is based on the parties' representations and agreement to facilitate discovery and is not a judicial determination that any particular material qualifies for protection under Rule 26(c) or otherwise.” (¶ 20, at 15–16)

Limits

A stipulation binds the parties. It does not make material work product as against non-parties or bind another court, and ¶ 20 says entry is not a judicial determination that material qualifies for protection. The first "deemed" sentence covers only outputs derived from designated information. The second sentence lists prompts and other materials created "in connection with this Action." It is unclear whether that extends protection to AI work that involves no designated information. The paragraph preserves Rule 26(a)(2) expert disclosure and the court's good-cause power. "Publicly accessible" and "permissible" are undefined.

Hintermaier v. Equifax Information Services, LLC

AI clause kept over pro se objection
S.D.N.Y. · Oct. 15, 2026 · Tarnofsky, M.J.

A magistrate judge refused to let a pro se plaintiff delete the AI paragraph (¶ 16) from a protective order the court had already approved. That paragraph bars any party from putting confidential information into an AI tool unless the provider is contractually barred from using the inputs for anything beyond the requested service (including training) and from disclosing them to third parties except where essential to the service. The judge's stated reason was that inputting another party's confidential information without those restrictions "creates an unacceptable risk that the information will be publicly disclosed."

Hintermaier v. Equifax Info. Servs., LLC, No. 24-cv-09697 (ALC) (RFT), ECF No. 172 (S.D.N.Y. Oct. 15, 2026) (Tarnofsky, M.J.). The order is signed "DATED: October 15, 2026"; the ECF header reads "Filed 10/14/26."

Why it matters

A New York federal court keeping an AI clause over a pro se litigant's objection. The test is contractual (no training, no third-party disclosure), not brand-based, and the leverage is real: sign it or get only non-confidential documents.

Facts

Mirela Hintermaier, representing herself, sued Equifax Information Services and other defendants in the Southern District of New York. The case is assigned to Judge Carter, with discovery before Magistrate Judge Robyn F. Tarnofsky. Defendants proposed a confidentiality stipulation and protective order (ECF 163-1), and the court directed the parties to execute and file it (ECF 168). Plaintiff instead moved for entry of her own revised version (ECF 169), which, among other changes, removed the AI text from ¶ 16. In ECF 172 the court granted the motion in part and denied it in part. It accepted two changes: plaintiff's edits to ¶ 1 "to account for Plaintiff's pro se status," and edits to ¶ 6 reflecting the court's earlier ruling that only plaintiff may produce documents on an AEO basis. It rejected all other revisions and discussed the six "most problematic." The AI deletion is item 6. So the AI clause originated in defendants' proposed order, was approved by the court, and was then kept over the plaintiff's attempt to delete it.

Holding

The court refused the deletion and required the previously approved ¶ 16. ECF 172 does not quote ¶ 16; it paraphrases it. As paraphrased, ¶ 16 precludes any party from inputting confidential information into any AI tool unless the AI provider is contractually prohibited from both (a) using the inputs, or information derived from them, for any purpose other than providing the specific service requested, including training any model; and (b) disclosing the inputs, or derived information, to any third party, except where disclosure is essential to delivering the requested service. The court ordered defendants, by October 19, 2026, to give plaintiff the ECF 163-1 form, revised only in ¶¶ 1 and 6 and signed by defendants. If plaintiff wanted defendants to start producing confidential material, she had to sign that form and file it by October 20. Alternatively, she could decline to sign, in which case defendants' production obligations would be limited to non-confidential documents. The actual wording of ¶ 16 is in ECF 163-1.

Reasoning

The AI ruling rests on a single sentence of the judge's own reasoning: "Inputting another party's confidential information into an AI tool without the restrictions set forth herein creates an unacceptable risk that the information will be publicly disclosed." That is the court's statement in ECF 172, not the text of ¶ 16. The court did not discuss particular products, consumer versus enterprise tiers, or what compliance would cost a self-represented litigant. The rest of the order explains why the court rejected plaintiff's other changes. Her statement that she signed without "agree[ing] to any provision" suggested she did not intend to comply. Her carve-out for communications with regulators (¶ 2) was unnecessary because original ¶ 14 already protects disclosures required by law or subpoena. Her change to ¶ 5, putting the burden on the designating party to move, conflicted with ¶ 3. Seven days' notice before filing medical information (¶ 11) was impractical. Her ¶ 15 change on notice of government requests was unnecessary because the original already excused notice where the law prohibits it.

Key quotations

  • “In Paragraph 16, Plaintiff removed text precluding any party from inputting confidential information into any artificial intelligence (“AI”) tool unless the AI provider is contractually prohibited from both (a) using the inputs, or information derived from them, for any purpose other than providing the specific service requested, including using them to train any model; and (b) disclosing the inputs, or any information derived from them, to any third party, except where such disclosure is essential to delivering the requested service.” (at 3–4)
  • “Inputting another party’s confidential information into an AI tool without the restrictions set forth herein creates an unacceptable risk that the information will be publicly disclosed.” (at 4)
  • “Plaintiff may, if she chooses, decline to execute and file the proposed protective order, in which case Defendants’ production obligations will be limited to non-confidential documents.” (at 4)
  • “However, stating that Plaintiff was executing the document without agreeing to any provision suggests that Plaintiff is signing the proposed protective order without intending to comply with its terms, which is unacceptable.” (at 2)

Limits

Quote ¶ 16 only through the court's paraphrase, cited as ECF 172 at 3–4; the actual paragraph is in ECF 163-1. Attribute the "unacceptable risk" sentence to the court in ECF 172 at 4, not to ¶ 16. Use October 15, 2026 (the date signed) even though the ECF header and RECAP show October 14. This is a magistrate judge's ruling on the form of a protective order, not a privilege or work-product ruling. The order does not say whether plaintiff used or planned to use AI, or why she wanted the clause removed.

Later history

According to the docket, a stipulated protective order was entered on October 21, 2026 (ECF 175) on plaintiff's letter motion, and plaintiff filed an "Objection (non-motion)" (ECF 176) the same day. Whether ECF 175 contains ¶ 16 as approved, and what ECF 176 objects to, were not verified. Check both before describing the outcome.

United States v. Wilkes

"Consumer versions" wording
S.D.N.Y. · Oct. 15, 2026 · Halpern, J.

Wilkes is a White Plains consent protective order that uses the same "consumer (i.e., non-commercial)" label as Sarris. In other respects it follows the older form: it lists brand names, applies a vendor-level test, sets flat conditions, and requires removal at the end of the case. It binds only the defense.

United States v. Wilkes, No. 7:26-cr-452 (PMH), ECF No. 24 (S.D.N.Y. Oct. 15, 2026) (Halpern, J.)

Why it matters

Uses consumer / commercial vocabulary, and "consumer versions of" makes clear the brands themselves are not banned, but keeps the older vendor-level test and flat conditions.

Facts

Wilkes is a three-defendant prosecution (Jaquey Wilkes, Tyrone Stewart, and Leroy Simpson) before Judge Philip M. Halpern. The order was entered on the government's application with the consent of counsel. The government signed on October 6, 2026, and the court so-ordered it on October 15. Each page carries a "2026.08.18" footer. The order has Disclosure, Sealed, APO, and AEO categories, and ¶ 9 is the AI provision.

Holding

Paragraph 9(a) defines "AI tool" broadly, with no exclusions. Paragraph 9(b) bars the defense team from submitting Disclosure Materials to "a consumer (i.e., non-commercial) version of an AI tool," giving as examples "consumer versions of" eight named products. It also bars any tool whose provider or vendor uses submitted data for model training, allows public access, discloses data to third parties, or, except as specified below, retains it. Paragraph 9(c) permits a tool that maintains confidentiality, will not expose materials to unauthorized third parties, and will not retain or use them for training, with all materials "removed from the AI tool(s) at the conclusion of this case." There is no restriction on the government.

Reasoning

As a consent order, it contains no reasoning beyond ¶ 9(b)'s sentence that such tools pose unique risks given the practical inability to claw back data once it is in a model. Compared with Sarris, Wilkes shares only the "consumer (i.e., non-commercial)" label. It lacks Sarris's definition of consumer versions by terms of service, the "terms or account settings" test, the "reasonable steps to confirm" standard, the "required by law" exception, the delete-or-request-deletion mechanism, and the exclusions for local software and embedded features. It does improve on the "publicly accessible" form by saying "consumer versions of" the named products. That makes clear that the brands themselves are not banned.

Key quotations

  • “The defense team will not submit Disclosure Materials to a consumer (i.e., non-commercial) version of an AI tool (e.g., consumer versions of ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity, Midjourney, Canva).” (¶ 9(b))
  • “Such AI tools pose unique risks to the security and integrity of Disclosure Materials, given the practical inability to claw back or delete data once it has been incorporated into a model.” (¶ 9(b))
  • “The defense may submit Disclosure Materials to an AI tool (i) that maintains the confidentiality of Disclosure Materials; (ii) that will not expose any Disclosure Materials to third parties not authorized under this Protective Order to receive such materials; (iii) that will not retain or use any Disclosure Materials for model training; and (iv) all Disclosure Materials will be removed from the AI tool(s) at the conclusion of this case.” (¶ 9(c))

Limits

A consent order, not a ruling. The origin of its consumer wording is not known from the text, and the signature-page scan is partly illegible.

Fortune v. Vivendi Ticketing US LLC

Free vs. paid line
S.D.N.Y. · Oct. 16, 2026 · Cronan, J.

Fortune is a stipulated S.D.N.Y. protective order in a putative class action against See Tickets. Its AI section bars using any produced material to train AI models. It also bars putting designated material into "any open source or otherwise free/unpaid" AI chatbot or model "such as ChatGPT," while expressly preserving paid, vendor-contracted tools and TAR. It draws a free-versus-paid line rather than a consumer-versus-enterprise line.

Fortune v. Vivendi Ticketing US, LLC, No. 1:24-cv-08415-JPC, ECF No. 39 (S.D.N.Y. Oct. 16, 2026) (Cronan, J.)

Why it matters

A third way to draw the line: free versus paid tools, plus a no-training rule for all produced material.

Facts

Solomon Fortune, individually and on behalf of a putative class, sued Vivendi Ticketing US, LLC d/b/a See Tickets USA. Counsel for both sides (Bursor & Fisher for plaintiff; Orrick for defendant) signed a stipulated protective order on October 15, 2026. Judge Cronan so-ordered it on October 16. Section 11 is titled "Use of Artificial Intelligence."

Holding

Section 11 has three parts. (1) No Training. The receiving party may not use any produced or exchanged material, designated or not, for prompts, completions, or embeddings used to train, improve, or otherwise develop any AI model. This applies whether the model is proprietary, open-source, or third-party, and whatever the purpose. Parties must take reasonable steps to make their employees, counsel, consultants, and others comply, and violations may draw sanctions. (2) No use of protected material in public or open-source AI models. Designated material may not be used for prompts, completions, or embeddings in "any open source or otherwise free/unpaid" AI chatbot, foundation model, or system "such as ChatGPT." The same reasonable-steps and sanctions language applies. (3) Savings clauses. Nothing limits use of commercially available TAR and other tools provided under paid contract through a reputable vendor, "conceptual-based queries" using AI models, or a party's ability to fully and fairly litigate.

Reasoning

Because the order is stipulated, it contains no reasoning. Its structure separates two risks. Training is barred for all produced material, whatever the tool or tier. Exposure through free or open-source tools is barred for designated material only. Rather than listing criteria such as deletion or no retention, it relies on a payment-and-contract line, plus an express savings clause for paid vendor tools.

Key quotations

  • “The Parties agree that the Receiving Party shall not use any documents, electronically stored information, or other materials produced or exchanged in the course of this litigation, including but not limited to those designated as “CONFIDENTIAL” or “HIGHLY CONFIDENTIAL – ATTORNEYS’ EYES ONLY” under any operative protective order, in whole or in part, for prompts (inputs), completions (outputs), or embeddings that are used to train, improve, re-train, or otherwise develop any artificial intelligence, foundation model, or large language model (collectively “AI Models”).” (¶ 11, at 15)
  • “A Receiving Party shall not use any documents, electronically stored information, or other materials designated as “CONFIDENTIAL” or “HIGHLY CONFIDENTIAL – ATTORNEYS’ EYES ONLY” in whole or in part, for prompts (inputs), completions (outputs), or embeddings in any open source or otherwise free/unpaid artificial intelligence chat bot, foundation model or system such as ChatGPT.” (¶ 11, at 15)
  • “Nothing in this Section shall limit a Receiving Party’s ability to use commercially available TAR and other tools provided under paid contract through a reputable vendor.” (¶ 11, at 16)
  • “Nothing in this Section shall limit a Receiving Party’s ability to perform conceptual-based queries using AI Models.” (¶ 11, at 16)

Limits

"Such as ChatGPT" names a product that comes in free, paid individual, and business versions. Read with "free/unpaid," it most naturally means the free version, but the text does not say so. "Open source" could be read to reach locally run open-source models, which pose little exposure risk. "Conceptual-based queries" is undefined. As a stipulated order, it contains no judicial analysis.

Fischman v. Epic Systems Corp.

AI safeguards approved over pro se objection
N.D. Tex. · Oct. 23, 2026 · Fitzwater, J.

In a pro se suit over the alleged role of Epic's medical software in the plaintiff's father's death, the court granted Epic's protective order, including a bar on putting confidential discovery into any AI tool unless the tool is contractually prohibited from storing or using inputs to train or improve a model and from disclosing them to third parties. It found the clause "nearly identical" to Morgan's and noted the order expressly preserves AI use for research, drafting and other tasks that do not involve protected material.

Fischman v. Epic Systems Corp., No. 3:26-cv-00770-D, ECF No. 57 (N.D. Tex. Oct. 23, 2026) (Fitzwater, J.) (memorandum opinion and order); entered protective order, ECF No. 58.

Why it matters

Morgan's clause, adopted by a senior district judge in a reasoned opinion, with the pro se access concern answered by carving out AI tasks that do not involve protected material.

Facts

Gallit Fischman, representing herself, served extensive discovery on the design and implementation of Epic's software. Epic moved for a protective order, supported by a declaration that the material is proprietary and that Epic instructs its own employees not to put such information into unapproved AI tools. Fischman acknowledged using AI in the litigation and objected to the AI provisions as broader than necessary.

Holding

Motion granted; the court entered Epic's revised order (ECF 58). The AI objection was overruled because Fischman did not explain how the provisions were overbroad, the order is "nearly identical to what other federal courts have approved," and she represented that she had obtained AI tools intended to meet Epic's requirements. The entered order also requires contractual deletion rights, documentation of the protections relied on, identification of the tool on request, and a pause on disputed tools pending agreement or a ruling.

Reasoning

Epic showed good cause with a particular and specific factual showing. The order limits how information is handled, not the scope of discovery, and expressly does not deny a pro se party access to discovery. Epic argued that entering information into generic AI tools is "effectively irreversible" because models commonly retain and train on submissions; given Fischman's acknowledged AI use, that was good cause for the AI restrictions.

Key quotations

  • “[I]t prevents the input of confidential and highly confidential information into an AI tool unless that tool is ‘contractually prohibited from . . . storing or using the inputs to train or improve any model’ and prohibited from ‘disclosing the inputs to any third party[.]’” (ECF 57 at 5)
  • “Given Fischman’s acknowledged use of AI in this litigation, . . . the court finds that there is good cause to impose Epic’s proposed AI restrictions in the protective order.” (ECF 57 at 7)
  • “Indeed, the proposed protective order is nearly identical to what other federal courts have approved.” (ECF 57 at 9)

Limits

A protective-order ruling about the other side's confidential information; it does not decide privilege or waiver over the pro se party's own AI work. The court's descriptions of AI products do not verify any vendor's current terms.
